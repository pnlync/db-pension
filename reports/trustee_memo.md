# Synthetic Scheme IE-1: funding position and options

To: the trustees (illustrative). Valuation date: 31 December 2025. Amounts in EUR m.

> All member data and member experience are synthetic. Market data are public and dated. Illustrative implementation based on published Pensions Authority and Society of Actuaries in Ireland guidance; not an actuarial certification or regulatory filing.

## 1. Purpose and headline numbers

This note sets out where the scheme stands on the two liability measures that matter to you and to the sponsor, what changed in 2025, and three ways of restoring the funding standard reserve (FSR).

| | 31 December 2025 |
|---|---|
| Assets | 204.7 |
| IAS 19 defined benefit obligation (company accounts) | 206.8 |
| Funding Standard liability (statutory wind-up test) | 203.0 |
| Funding Standard funding level | 100.8% (met) |
| Funding standard reserve (FSR) | 13.2 |
| Assets / (Funding Standard + FSR) | 94.7% (not met; shortfall 11.5) |

The scheme meets the Funding Standard but not the Funding Standard plus the FSR, so a funding proposal is needed.

## 2. Why the two liability measures differ

Both measures value the same 992 members' benefits but answer different questions. IAS 19 is a going-concern, best-estimate measure: salaries are projected to retirement and cash flows are discounted at AA corporate bond yields (single equivalent rate 4.37%). The Funding Standard asks whether the scheme could meet accrued benefits if it wound up today: active members are treated as leaving now, non-pensioners are valued as statutory transfer values (6% before 65 and 4.25% after, with the market value adjustment) and pensioners at the cost of buying annuities.

The Funding Standard liability is 3.8 below the IAS 19 DBO, but that small net gap hides large offsetting steps: the statutory basis takes 18.8 off the non-pensioners (today's salary instead of salary at exit: 10.1; 1.5% increases: 6.5; statutory discounting: 2.5), while annuity pricing adds 11.1 for pensioners and wind-up expenses add 4.0. Meeting the Funding Standard therefore does not mean the scheme is secure on an accounting or buy-out view.

## 3. What happened in 2025

Euro yields rose during 2025 and the IAS 19 discount rate moved from 3.51% to 4.37%. The DBO fell from 238.5 to 206.8, and the IAS 19 deficit from 33.2 to 2.1. The change in the discount curve alone reduced the DBO by 32.2; the same rise in yields cut the value of the bond portfolio, so assets returned 3.7 less than the IAS 19 interest credit. The 2025 charge to profit or loss was 4.3 and the other comprehensive income gain 31.0. On the statutory basis the funding level improved from 97.4% to 100.8%. Member experience was close to the assumptions (synthetic data).

## 4. Main risks

The same portfolio hedges 32% of the interest-rate sensitivity of the IAS 19 liability but 67% of the Funding Standard liability, because statutory transfer values move with rates only within ten years of retirement. A 0.5% fall in yields would lower the IAS 19 funding level by 5.2 percentage points and the Funding Standard level by 1.2. Inflation, a 20% fall in equities and a one-year rise in life expectancy are the other material risks (see the tornado chart).

## 5. Options

| Option | Deficit contributions a year | FS + FSR cover now | Cover after 3 years | IAS 19 / FS hedge ratio |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Contributions only | 4.5 | 94.7% | 100% | 32% / 67% |
| Move 20% from equities to long euro sovereigns only | 0 | 98.0% | 94.5% | 52% / 106% |
| Switch and contributions | 3.5 | 98.0% | 100% | 52% / 106% |

- **Contributions** close the gap without changing risk. Three years is the base case; a ten-year plan would need 2.5 a year (a strategic planning scenario, not a proposed statutory recovery period).
- **The switch** cuts the FSR shortfall by 64% to 4.1, because sovereign bonds count against both parts of the reserve, and reduces the IAS 19 hit from a 0.5% fall in yields to 3.7 points. It gives up about 1.3 a year of expected return, so on its own the cover drifts down again, and over ten years it costs more than it saves (3.2 a year against 2.5).
- **A pensioner buy-in** at the illustrative price of 134.4 (117% of the pensioners' IAS 19 value; Solvency II technical provisions 121%) would remove the pensioners' longevity risk and 83% of the Funding Standard rate sensitivity, and cut the FSR to 3.6. But the price is 7% above the annuity cost used in the Funding Standard, so the Funding Standard level would fall to 93.9%; the company would book an IAS 19 loss of 19.9 in other comprehensive income (assuming a qualifying policy that exactly matches the insured benefits) and its funding level would fall to 89.4%. Paying by selling equities rather than sovereign bonds keeps the qualifying assets and leaves a shortfall of 4.7.

## 6. Recommendation (within this synthetic scenario)

Adopt the switch together with deficit contributions of 3.5 a year, which restores the funding standard plus reserve within three years at the lowest cash cost, and review the investment strategy again before committing to it for longer, because the lost equity return outweighs the lower reserve over a ten-year horizon. Do not proceed with a pensioner buy-in on the illustrative terms: seek insurer quotations, calibrate the Funding Standard annuity basis to them, and fund any transaction from equities.

## 7. Limitations

Member data and experience are synthetic and the benefits are simplified (no spouses' pensions, early retirement or commutation). The IAS 19 curve is ECB AAA plus a calibrated spread; inflation is proxied by French 2032 government bonds rather than the euro inflation swap curve; timing is annual; inflation is deterministic, which understates the value of the 3% cap and 0% floor. The annuity cost, insurer loadings and buy-in price are illustrative. The statutory recovery period follows the Pensions Act and is not modelled. The Funding Standard liability for non-pensioners is not the cost of a guaranteed deferred-annuity buy-out.
